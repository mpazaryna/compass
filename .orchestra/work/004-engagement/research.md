# Canonical Grounding for the "Centering My Business" Curriculum

Input to the spec. For each of the five bearings: what the standard business
curriculum actually teaches on that territory, the question set it teaches, and
what earns a stage.

The premise, stated plainly: the questions a business program teaches an owner to
ask are *for owners*. Sheri is one. Nothing about them is proprietary or hard —
what is scarce is being asked them, in order, by something that won't let you
skip. That is the whole delivery problem Compass exists to solve, and it is why
these bearings are question sets rather than lessons.

**Two findings changed the scope.** Both of the bearings previously flagged as
thin — life cycle and anticipating change — were thin because they were grounded
in the wrong source. Better sources make both of them genuinely ordered.

---

## 1. Life cycle

**Grounding: Churchill & Lewis, "The Five Stages of Small-Business Growth"
(HBR, May 1983).**

The workbook's stage names track Adizes' corporate life cycle, and the obvious
academic upgrade looks like Greiner (HBR 1972) — but Greiner is the wrong
instrument here. Greiner's five phases describe an organization growing in
*headcount and management layers*: creativity → leadership crisis → direction →
autonomy crisis → delegation → control crisis → coordination → red-tape crisis →
collaboration. A single-location salon lives entirely inside Greiner's first two
phases. Four of his five transitions are unreachable.

Churchill & Lewis is the small-business model, and it fits: **Existence**
(getting customers, delivering the service), **Survival** (is there enough money
to break even and stay open), **Success** (the real fork — exploit what's been
built and expand, or hold it stable and profitable as a base for other things),
**Take-off** (grow fast, and finance it), **Resource Maturity**.

**What earns a stage.** The Success-stage fork is the bearing. It is not a
diagnosis, it is a decision with consequences — and it is precisely the question
an established salon owner is actually sitting on. Sequence: place the business →
test the placement against evidence → name what the *next* stage demands →
decide, at the fork, which way she is going and why.

This resolves the earlier objection that life cycle collapses to a two-question
diagnosis. Placement is the setup; the fork is the work.

## 2. Know your numbers

**Grounding: standard financial statement analysis, taught as three statements
that only mean something together.**

The teachable thing is not any single statement — it is the contradiction
between them. In a single month a business can show profit on the income
statement, rising debt on the balance sheet, and cash leaving faster than it
arrives on the cash flow statement, all at once. Each report alone is
incomplete.

The question each statement answers:

- **Income statement** — *are we making money?* Do current prices cover costs and
  still support owner pay and reinvestment? Is net margin stable, rising, or
  compressing? Have top expense categories moved without a reason?
- **Balance sheet** — *what is our position right now?* Is the business building
  value or carrying too much debt? How many months of runway if revenue paused
  tomorrow? What's sitting past 30 days in receivables?
- **Cash flow** — *where did cash actually move?* Is cash from core operations
  positive?

**Salon anchor:** a healthy hair-salon EBITDA margin runs roughly 10–18%; below
that usually means rent or payroll is too high.

**What earns a stage.** One stage per statement, in that order, then a fourth
that puts all three side by side and asks what they disagree about. Cash last,
because it's the one that closes businesses, and it only lands after the other
two have been read.

## 3. Growth multipliers

**Grounding: customer equity / CLV decomposition (Gupta & Lehmann, *Managing
Customers as Investments*, 2005).**

Abraham's "three ways to grow" is the plain-language version of a decomposition
the literature treats as customer equity: customers are assets, and marketing
spend on keeping them is an investment rather than an expense. Revenue =
customers × frequency × average value is the standard retail decomposition of
that idea.

**This is the bearing where salon benchmarks make the gates checkable**, and
they are unusually good:

| Metric | Benchmark |
|---|---|
| Existing-client retention | 65–80% |
| New-client retention | 40–60% |
| Rebooking / prebook rate | 65–85%, aim 50%+ prebooked |
| Prebooked vs. not | ~70% retain vs. ~30% |
| Established stylist annual service revenue | $60K–$120K |

Rebooking is reported as the single most predictive retention metric — which
makes it the number this bearing should drive toward, not average ticket. Note
also that average-ticket growth is generally attributed to service upgrades and
retail attachment rather than price increases; a stage that asks "raise prices"
is asking the wrong question.

**What earns a stage.** Her three real numbers → which lever is genuinely
cheapest *for her* (for a salon it is almost always frequency, via rebooking) →
a target → the compounded result → her reasons for doing the work. The last one
is not decoration: without it this bearing is three numbers and a multiplication,
which is exactly the "answer two questions, get a doc" collapse.

## 4. The funnel

**Grounding: Goldratt's Theory of Constraints — the Five Focusing Steps — with
the purchase funnel as the map it is applied to.**

The funnel itself is old and descriptive: AIDA (Awareness, Interest, Desire,
Action) was developed by E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1898 from life-insurance selling;
Townsend married it to the funnel metaphor in 1924. It tells her the shape, not
what to do.

The procedure is TOC: **Identify** the constraint → **Exploit** it (get more from
it without spending) → **Subordinate** everything else to it → **Elevate** it
(now spend) → **Repeat**, because the constraint moves.

**What earns a stage.** All five steps, in order, and the order is the entire
value — the discipline is *exploit before elevate*, which is exactly right for an
owner with no capital to throw at the problem. Most owners skip to elevate. A
gate that will not let her spend before she has proven she got everything free
from the constraint is the sharpest gate in the curriculum.

This also settles the overlap question with bearing 3: the multipliers bearing
produces the numbers; this one finds which one is the constraint and works it.
Different question, same data.

## 5. Anticipating change

**Grounding: Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem" (HBR, September 2007) —
not PESTEL.**

PESTEL and Porter's Five Forces are the obvious grounding and the wrong one for
this bearing. Both are analytical *checklists*: breadth without sequence, and
you can answer their categories in any order without losing anything. That is
exactly why this bearing read as thin.

Klein's premortem is a procedure. Rather than asking what might go wrong, it
asserts that the thing has already failed and asks what *did* go wrong.
Prospective hindsight — imagining an outcome has already occurred — is reported
to improve the ability to correctly identify reasons for it by around 30%. It
also counters overconfidence, which is the specific failure mode of an owner
doing this alone.

**What earns a stage.** State the plan (the artifacts from bearings 1–4 are the
plan) → assert it is eighteen months later and it failed → write causes
independently before evaluating any → sort them by which she can actually affect
→ pair the top ones with a response. PESTEL survives only as an optional prompt
list if she runs dry generating causes.

**Placement note:** this bearing consumes the outputs of the other four, which
argues for it staying last in the curriculum.

## What this changes in the PRD

1. **Scope framing.** The source is the standard curriculum, cited per bearing.
   The workbook is what prompted the survey, not the map the scope was drawn
   from. Note 8's provenance caution mostly falls away.
2. **The order is now argued, not proposed.** Life cycle first (where am I),
   numbers second (what is true), multipliers third (what moves), funnel fourth
   (which one, and how), premortem last (what kills it) — because the last one
   takes the first four as its input.
3. **"What makes a gate checkable" has an answer for at least two bearings.**
   The salon benchmarks in §3 and the exploit-before-elevate rule in §4 are
   external standards a gate can be written against, rather than asking her to
   declare herself done.
4. **Each bearing's README cites its grounding.** That is what makes the catalog
   defensible when a client asks where this came from — and it never reaches the
   client, per the bearing-README convention.

## Confidence

Everything cited above was verified against sources this session and is listed
below. Two frameworks are named from memory and are *not* relied on for scope —
Adizes' corporate life cycle (as the thing the workbook's stage names track) and
Porter's "What Is Strategy?" (as a possible grounding for differentiation, which
is not currently a bearing). Verify before either appears in client-facing copy.
